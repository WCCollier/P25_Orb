ARC Edge · P25 Orb · Drawing set, plates 2–4

The three drawings that carry the questions

Each of these answers a question the hardware document argues in prose and has never shown: where in the band the two windows sit, why the antennas are arranged the way they are, and why a 3.2 dB loss costs almost nothing.

Plate 2 — The 800 MHz duplex band and the two captured windows

Drawn at true scale, with the two windows magnified below. This is the drawing that makes the two-group architecture obvious rather than arguable — and it is the one that was got wrong twice.

§3.3
The two outlined notches on the true-scale bar are the entire capture. Everything else in the 800 MHz band is invisible to the receiver at any given moment. Seeing how narrow they are is what makes the intermodulation argument land: each group sees a handful of signals rather than a whole duplex band.
8TAC95D
Drawn in the uplink colour inside the downlink window, because that is exactly what it is — a handset transmission sitting 337.5 kHz from the control channel. It is simplex, so it has no repeater pair and no uplink counterpart. This is the single detail most likely to be probed, and the honest answer is that the Texas plan sited it there; the design merely benefits.
45.34 MHz
The forcing number. A handset's emissions run from 806.2125 to 851.5500, so one coherent group covering all of them needs at least that much instantaneous bandwidth. The ADRV9002's 40 MHz cannot reach it, which is what turns a preference into arithmetic.

Plate 3 — Array geometry

Two views: the triangle the direction finding depends on, and how it sits on the lid once the case is open. The band strip underneath is the constraint that decides the enclosure.

§5.1
The three elements must be non-collinear in the horizontal plane, not merely non-collinear in space. Put all three in a vertical plane and azimuth enters the phase measurement only through cos φ — and cos φ equals cos(−φ), so two signals arriving from mirrored azimuths produce identical phase on every baseline. Adding elements does not help; the ambiguity belongs to the plane.
λ/2
Spacing above half a wavelength makes the phase measurement ambiguous. Spacing below it does not — it only costs accuracy, and predictably, with bearing error scaling roughly as λ/d. That is why UHF at 0.27λ is degraded rather than broken, and why VHF fails on element size rather than on ambiguity.

Plate 4 — Why a 3.2 dB splitter costs 0.04 dB

The smallest drawing here and the one most likely to be challenged directly, because the claim sounds wrong until you see where the gain sits.

§3.3
Figures are Inferred from Friis, assuming a 1.0 dB / 20 dB LNA. The linear terms on the plate are shown rather than hidden so the arithmetic can be checked live: 1.259 is 1.0 dB as a ratio, 2.089 is 3.2 dB as a ratio, and 100 is 20 dB of gain.
Second benefit
Splitting after the amplifier also means both coherent groups see the same amplifier for a given element. That element's amplifier phase becomes common to both paths instead of being two independent error sources — which matters because phase is the measurement.
Scope
The LNAs already live in the processing body and the dock sits before them, so this is a processing-body change only. No new band-module SKU, no antenna rework.

All three plates are conceptual and pitch-quality, not procurement-ready. Dimensions are design intent; the case-fit conclusions in Plate 3 are Inferred from approximate interior dimensions and have not been measured against a real enclosure.
